# All marriages between Party members had to be approved by a committee

All marriages between Party members had to be approved by a committee -- and the approval was designed to be withheld precisely when the applicants looked like lovers. In the world of Nineteen Eighty-Four this rule was the administrative face of a deeper campaign: the Party's real, undeclared purpose was to remove all pleasure from the sexual act, and eroticism rather than love was the enemy, inside marriage as much as outside it. The only recognised purpose of marriage was to beget children for the service of the Party, and sexual intercourse was to be regarded as a slightly disgusting minor operation, like an enema. Because the principle was never clearly stated, it had to be rubbed into every Party member from childhood onwards.

## The Rule and Its Declared Purpose

The committee itself was appointed for the express purpose of passing judgment on Party marriages, and its criterion inverted every romantic expectation: permission was always refused if the couple gave the impression of being physically attracted to one another. Marriage was thus stripped of desire and reconceived as a contribution of labour to the State, an arrangement closer to a production quota than to a personal bond. The aim was not merely to prevent men and women from forming loyalties the Party could not control; it was to make sure that no loyalty worth the name could form at all.

The rule operated in a legal void. Nothing was illegal in Oceania, because there were no longer any laws, yet a Party member who kept a private diary knew that detection meant death, or at least twenty-five years in a forced-labour camp. Prohibitions lived not in statutes but in the arbitrary power of the Party, which demanded not only right opinions but right instincts. A naturally orthodox member -- a goodthinker, in Newspeak -- would know without thinking what the desirable emotion was; the rest were shaped by a mental training grouped round the Newspeak disciplines of crimestop, blackwhite and doublethink.

## Surveillance and Enforcement

From birth to death a Party member lived under the eye of the Thought Police. Even alone he could never be sure that he was alone; friendships, relaxations, behaviour towards wife and children, the expression of the face, the words muttered in sleep, even characteristic movements of the body were scrutinised. The marriage committee was only one node in this apparatus. Behind it stood the Ministry of Love, the windowless building Winston Smith had never approached within half a kilometre, ringed with barbed-wire entanglements, steel doors and hidden machine-gun nests, and patrolled by guards in black uniforms carrying jointed truncheons. The purges, arrests, tortures and vaporisations that flowed from such institutions were not punishments for crimes already committed but the wiping-out of persons who might at some future time commit a crime.

Winston's own marriage to Katharine demonstrated what the system produced. The union had been contracted -- and, in its way, endured -- in the spirit the committee required: a weekly, ritualised act of "making a baby" that Katharine herself called "our duty to the Party", performed without pleasure and broken off when no child appeared. In this personal case the rule's true purpose could be seen: not to bind two people but to keep them apart, and to turn the most intimate act into a testimony of obedience.

## Consequences and Significance

The committee's power pointed toward a future O'Brien described from the Ministry of Love: no wives and no friends, children taken from their mothers at birth, the sex instinct eradicated, procreation reduced to an annual formality, the orgasm abolished. "The object of persecution is persecution. The object of torture is torture. The object of power is power." The marriage rule was the first instalment of that programme, a legal-looking gate erected at the entrance to private life so that the Party could control what lay beyond.

Orwell's own politics made the rule legible as an indictment rather than a blueprint. He had defined his post-1936 writing as a struggle against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, and he was particularly angered by left-wing intellectuals who accepted the Stalinist regime as socialist while silently recognising that its spirit and practice were alien to anything meant by socialism in England; that "schizophrenic manner of thinking" allowed such things as concentration camps and mass deportations to be right and wrong simultaneously. The marriage committee belongs to this analysis: a system that speaks of duty, family and reproduction while engineering the destruction of all three.

The final proof of the system's success was not a marriage at all but the ending of Winston's story: in the Chestnut Tree Café, after torture and re-education, he looked up at the enormous face of Big Brother and felt that he had won the victory over himself. "He loved Big Brother." The committee that had policed physical attraction between Party members had prepared the ground for a world in which love itself was redirected to the State. Even the rewriting of memory, the endless falsification that the Ministry of Truth carried out on the past, was part of the same project: if history could be scraped clean and re-inscribed, so could the human heart.
