# Encounter with a prostitute

In his diary, Winston Smith records a sexual encounter with a prostitute that took place three years earlier, an act of clandestine rebellion against the Party's systematic suppression of erotic pleasure. The episode, which he forces himself to write down despite intense revulsion, becomes a mirror for his own degraded condition and the impossibility of authentic intimacy under the Party's rule.

## The Diary Entry as Confession

The urge to confess the encounter drives Winston to his diary, yet the memory torments him so violently that he feels an impulse to shout obscenities or smash objects. He recognizes that the tension in his nervous system could betray him through visible symptoms, recalling a man whose facial spasm signalled certain doom. The act of writing is itself a dangerous risk, since the diary is a compromising possession that could lead to death or twenty-five years in a forced-labour camp. Nevertheless, he forces himself to set down the details, knowing that the thoughtcrime is already irrevocable.

## The Sordid Encounter

The encounter occurred on a dark evening in a narrow side-street near a railway station. A woman with a thickly painted face—white mask-like paint and bright red lips, a style no Party woman would use—stood under a dim street lamp. She asked for two dollars. Winston followed her through a doorway, across a backyard, into a basement kitchen where a low lamp burned beside a bed. The smell of bugs and cheap scent, alluring precisely because it was forbidden, filled the room. When he turned up the lamp, he saw her clearly for the first time: she was an old woman, at least fifty, with streaks of white in her hair and a toothless mouth that gaped like a cavern. Despite the horror, he went ahead and completed the act. It was his first lapse in two years, a violation of the Party's prohibition against consorting with prostitutes, punishable by five years in a forced-labour camp.

## Contrast with Katharine and Party Ideology

The memory of the prostitute immediately evokes his wife, Katharine, who embodies the Party's sexual doctrine. Their marriage, approved by a committee, was a duty-bound arrangement intended only to produce children for the Party. Katharine herself was a 'human sound-track', utterly devoid of original thought, and their sexual encounters were a weekly ritual she called 'making a baby' or 'our duty to the Party'. She would lie stiff and unresponsive, pushing him away even as she submitted. Winston reflects that the Party's real aim is to kill the sex instinct, to remove all pleasure from the sexual act, and to treat intercourse as a slightly disgusting minor operation. The Party encourages prostitution among the proles as a furtive, joyless outlet, but the unforgivable crime is any genuine passion between Party members. Winston's encounter with the prostitute, and his inability to find a real love affair, is a direct consequence of this system.

## The Meaning of the Transgression

For Winston, the sexual act successfully performed is rebellion, and desire itself is thoughtcrime. Yet the encounter leaves him with a feeling of defeat and resentment, mixed with the image of Katharine's frozen body. The prostitute's aged, toothless face reveals the degradation to which he has been reduced; he is unable to have a woman of his own and must resort to these filthy scuffles at intervals of years. The memory of the cheap scent is inextricably linked to fornication, and the whole experience is a symptom of the Party's success in distorting human nature. Writing it down brings no catharsis; the urge to shout filthy words remains as strong as ever. The episode stands as a testament to the impossibility of maintaining a private self under total surveillance, and to the Party's power to corrupt even the most intimate acts.
