# Final conversion

The final conversion is the terminal event of Nineteen Eighty-Four, the moment at which Winston Smith, released from the Ministry of Love, sits in the near-empty Chestnut Tree Café and learns what kind of smile is hidden beneath Big Brother's dark moustache. As the afternoon bulletin announces a vast Eurasian rout, the last equivocation in his heart dissolves, and the novel ends with the words "He loved Big Brother." The scene completes the Party's project of remaking him from within, turning pain and re-education into what is presented as Winston's own final victory over himself.

## Background and Design

The conversion is the logical end of the theory of power that O'Brien expounds during Winston's re-education. "The Party seeks power entirely for its own sake," O'Brien tells him; power is not a means but an end. Its real instrument is not wealth or luxury but the remaking of human minds — "tearing human minds to pieces and putting them together again in new shapes of your own choosing" — so that the Party can create its own human nature. Thomas Pynchon, introducing the novel, links this programme to the era's fear of Pavlovian brainwashing: readers who took Nineteen Eighty-Four as a condemnation of Stalinist atrocity found in Winston's ordeal an extended portrait of political reflexes conditioned into the subject. The novel had already shown how such conditioning worked in the Two Minutes Hate, where a hideous ecstasy of fear and vindictiveness flowed through the group and Winston's hatred could be switched from one object to another "like the flame of a blowlamp," and in the discipline of doublethink, which taught the mind "to know and not to know" and to hold two cancelled-out opinions at once. All these currents converge on the café scene.

## The Scene of Surrender

The Chestnut Tree Café at the lonely hour of fifteen is almost empty; sunlight falls yellow on dusty table-tops and tinny music trickles from the telescreens. Winston sits in his usual corner, gazing into an empty glass, and the waiters know his habits: his corner table is always reserved, the chessboard is always waiting, and the gin bottle appears unbidden. Since his release he has grown fatter and regained his colour, the skin of nose and cheekbones is coarsely red, even the bald scalp is too deep a pink; the chess problem before him — "White to play and mate in two moves" — strikes him with a cloudy mysticism, because white always mates, as Good always triumphs over Evil. On the dusty table he has traced the figures 2+2=5.

All afternoon the news from the African front has disquieted him: a Eurasian army is moving south, Brazzaville and Leopoldville are in danger, and for the first time in the war the territory of Oceania itself is menaced. The telescreen warns him to stand by for an important announcement at fifteen-thirty. When the trumpet-call comes, it signals victory — a vast seaborne armada secretly assembled, a sudden blow in the enemy's rear, half a million prisoners, control of the whole of Africa. Cheering crowds fill the streets. Under the table Winston's feet make convulsive movements; in his mind he is running with the crowds, cheering himself deaf. Looking up at the portrait of Big Brother — "the colossus that bestrode the world," "the rock against which the hordes of Asia dashed themselves in vain" — he realises that only ten minutes earlier there had still been equivocation in his heart. "Much had changed in him since that first day in the Ministry of Love, but the final, indispensable, healing change had never happened, until this moment."

## The Death of Love

The conversion is sealed by the extinction of the one loyalty that had survived Room 101. The final meeting with Julia has already shown how completely the re-education has done its work. In the Park on a biting March day she is sallower, with a long scar across her forehead and a waist that has grown thicker and stiffened, so that her body feels like a corpse dragged from ruins. They exchange the same bald words — "I betrayed you" / "I betrayed you" — and she looks at him with contempt and dislike. He allows himself to be separated from her in the street and is chiefly relieved to get back to the warm café with its chessboard and ever-flowing gin; when he looks back after fifty metres he can no longer distinguish her among the hurrying figures. "They can't get inside you," Julia once promised, but in the café Winston knows they could get inside; O'Brien's words come back to him: "What happens to you here is for ever." Something has been killed in his breast, burnt out, cauterised out.

## The Victory over the Self

The final paragraphs turn the surrender into a triumph. Winston gazes up at the enormous face and reflects that it has taken him forty years to learn what kind of smile was hidden beneath the dark moustache. "O cruel, needless misunderstanding! O stubborn, self-willed exile from the loving breast!" Two gin-scented tears trickle down the sides of his nose. In his mind he is back in the Ministry of Love — confessing everything, implicating everybody, walking down the white-tiled corridor "with the feeling of walking in sunlight," the long-hoped-for bullet entering his brain. "But it was all right, everything was all right, the struggle was finished. He had won the victory over himself. He loved Big Brother." The narrative presents the conversion not as defeat but as the healing change that completes him, the moment when more than a Eurasian army perishes.

## Interpretation and Aftermath

Pynchon's introduction frames the scene within Cold War discourse of brainwashing, noting that something very much like it happens to Winston "in lengthy and terrifying detail" and that readers took the novel as a simple condemnation of Stalinist atrocity. Yet the appendix, "The Principles of Newspeak," is written in the past tense, hinting that Newspeak became a thing of the past and that "the ancient humanistic ways of thinking inherent in standard English have persisted, survived, and ultimately prevailed." The final conversion, in other words, is not quite the last word: the very language of the epilogue belongs to a future in which Winston's capitulation has been undone. Pynchon also reads the ending as darker than physical death — Winston and Julia have entered permanently the condition of doublethink, "no longer in love but able to hate and love Big Brother at the same time" — while the appendix's hints of restoration serve, in his view, to brighten an otherwise bleakly pessimistic ending. The café scene is thus both the completion of the Party's work and, in the book's own closing structure, the evidence that the work could not last.
