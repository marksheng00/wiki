# Freedom is the freedom to say that two plus two make four

The phrase 'Freedom is the freedom to say that two plus two make four' appears in Winston Smith's diary as a defiant assertion of objective truth against the Party's doctrine of doublethink. It is the fulcrum on which the novel's entire political argument turns: the belief that if even the simplest mathematical fact can be denied, then all truth is subject to the will of those in power, and freedom is reduced to the right to cling to reality itself.

## Origin and Definition

Winston writes the sentence after a long inner struggle with the Party's ideology. He has just spent the morning in the Records Department of the Ministry of Truth, falsifying the past, and later in the canteen he has witnessed the unquestioning acceptance of contradictory news. The diary entry is a private act of rebellion, performed in the knowledge that the Thought Police may detect and kill him for it. The line itself is a syllogism: if you can state what is objectively true—that two plus two equals four—without being punished, then you possess a basic freedom. But if the Party can force you to say it equals five, then freedom is lost. The formulation links political liberty to the integrity of logic and mathematics, grounding resistance in the simplest possible axiom.

## The Battle Over Objective Reality

The phrase recurs at the climax of Winston's interrogation in the Ministry of Love. O'Brien, strapping him to a chair and using a pain dial, forces Winston to count the fingers he holds up. When Winston insists they are four, O'Brien applies increasing pain, demanding that he say five. Winston finally screams 'Five! Five! Five!' while still believing they are four. Later, during his re-education, Winston writes on his slate 'TWO AND TWO MAKE FIVE,' attempting to internalize the Party's doctrine. But the old belief persists, and he feels a 'check' in his mind. The struggle between four and five becomes the final test of his surrender: the Party demands not just outward obedience but the extinction of the very concept of objective truth. In the end, Winston's victory over himself is achieved when he truly loves Big Brother, but the novel's appendix suggests that Newspeak, which would make such a thought impossible, never fully succeeded.

## Relation to Doublethink

The phrase encapsulates the mental gymnastics of doublethink, the Party's technique of believing two contradictory truths simultaneously. The Party's slogan 'Freedom is Slavery' is the symmetrical opposite of Winston's maxim. While Winston argues that freedom lies in acknowledging the self-evident, doublethink requires the citizen to believe that two plus two make five when the Party says so, and to forget that they ever believed otherwise. The condition is described in Goldstein's book as a 'power of holding two contradictory beliefs in one's mind simultaneously, and accepting both of them.' Winston's maxim is a direct challenge to this: it insists that there is a bedrock of fact that cannot be altered by any human institution. The conflict between the two positions is the novel's central philosophical drama.

## Significance as a Touchstone for Freedom

Winston's phrase has become an enduring symbol of intellectual resistance. It asserts that some truths are not negotiable, and that the freedom to declare them is the foundation of all other freedoms. The novel does not offer a happy ending—Winston is broken and learns to love Big Brother—but the fact that the phrase is written in the diary, and that the diary survives in some form to be read by future generations (as the Appendix implies), leaves open the possibility that the truth, once spoken, cannot be entirely erased. The line is a quiet, desperate affirmation of sanity in a world of organized madness, and it remains one of the most cited passages in twentieth-century literature.
