# Julia passes Winston a love note

In Nineteen Eighty-Four, the moment Julia passes Winston Smith a love note is the hinge between isolation and conspiracy, turning a suspected enemy into the novel's central ally. Inside the Ministry of Truth the whole handover lasts less than half a minute: while Winston helps Julia to her feet, a small flat scrap of paper, folded into a square, changes hands. When he finally dares to read it, the note says, in a large unformed handwriting, "I love you." The disclosure reverses Winston's long-held fear that the dark-haired girl from the Fiction Department is an agent of the Thought Police, and it sets in motion the clandestine love affair that becomes the novel's central rebellion.

## Background and Suspicion

For weeks beforehand, Winston has believed the girl dangerous. He knows that even if she is not a member of the Thought Police, the amateur spy is the greatest danger of all, and he has learned to treat every stray sign as a risk: a nervous tic, an unconscious look of anxiety, a habit of muttering to oneself; even wearing an improper expression on one's face is a punishable offence, an offence Newspeak calls facecrime. In his mind the girl from the Fiction Department already belongs to this category of menace, and the thought that she might have been sent to trap him is never far away.

## The Handover

On this morning Julia comes towards him along the corridor, stumbles and falls almost flat on her face. Winston stops short and helps her up, and in the two or three seconds of that contact she slips something into his hand; there is no question that she has done it intentionally. It is a scrap of paper folded into a square, small and flat, and as he passes through the lavatory door he transfers it to his pocket and feels it with the tips of his fingers.

He does not dare read it at once. While he stands at the urinal he manages to get it unfolded, but he resists the temptation to take it into one of the water-closets, because there is no place where one can be more certain that the telescreens are watched continuously. Back in his cubicle he throws the fragment casually among the other papers on his desk, puts on his spectacles and tells himself to wait at least five minutes. His heart bumps in his breast with frightening loudness; fortunately the work before him is mere routine, the rectification of a long list of figures, and he can keep his voice from trembling as he murmurs the figures into the speakwrite.

Even before the words are visible, he weighs two possibilities. The more likely, he thinks, is that she is an agent of the Thought Police and that the paper contains a threat, a summons, an order to commit suicide, or a trap of some description. The wilder possibility, which his intellect dismisses as absurd, is that the message comes from an underground organisation—perhaps the Brotherhood itself. Eight minutes after the paper reaches his desk he flattens it out and reads, in a large unformed handwriting, "I love you." For several seconds he is too stunned even to throw the incriminating thing into the memory hole; when he does, he cannot resist reading it once again, just to make sure the words are really there.

## Aftermath and Significance

The note transforms Julia from a suspected enemy into a desired and desiring rebel, and it gives Winston what his intellect had told him was impossible: hope. Her later explanation that she has always yelled with the crowd because it is "the only way to be safe" makes sense of the deception—the love note is the first deliberate crack in her camouflage, the one moment when her carefully maintained Party-girl surface is voluntarily dropped. In a world where the telescreen makes even the lavatory a watched space, the successful passing of the note proves that two people can still make contact under the Party's nose, and that Winston is not, after all, alone.
