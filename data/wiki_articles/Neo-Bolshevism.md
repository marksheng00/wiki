# Neo-Bolshevism

Neo-Bolshevism is the official philosophy of Eurasia, one of the three rival super-states in George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four, and the counterpart of Oceania's Ingsoc. It enters the novel through the forbidden testimony of Emmanuel Goldstein's The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism, where it is named alongside Eastasia's Death-Worship as one of a triad of ideologies described as "barely distinguishable," supporting social systems that are "not distinguishable at all." The concept's decisive significance is that it exposes the ideological enmity dividing the world as manufactured: the supposedly warring creeds are structural twins, and each super-state depends on the others' hated philosophies to make its own domination seem natural and necessary.

## Definition and Origin

In the historical sketch contained in Goldstein's book, Neo-Bolshevism belongs to a generation of new movements that appeared in the middle years of the century, alongside Ingsoc in Oceania and Death-Worship in Eastasia. These movements grew out of the older socialist tradition and retained its names, paying lip-service to its ideology, but their conscious aim was the opposite of emancipation: to perpetuate unfreedom and inequality, to arrest progress, and to freeze history at a chosen moment. By 1984 the process is complete. Eurasia's prevailing philosophy is Neo-Bolshevism, just as Oceania lives under Ingsoc and Eastasia under Death-Worship — the Chinese name usually translated as Death-Worship, the book notes, might better be rendered as Obliteration of the Self.

## The Doctrine and Its Function

The citizens of Oceania are forbidden to learn anything of the tenets of Neo-Bolshevism or its sister philosophies; they are taught only to execrate them as barbarous outrages upon morality and common sense. The book, however, insists that the actual content of the three creeds is virtually identical. Everywhere there is the same pyramidal structure, the same worship of a semi-divine leader, and the same economy existing by and for continuous warfare. In consequence the three super-states cannot conquer one another and would gain nothing by doing so; so long as they remain in conflict they prop one another up, and their permanent war is at bottom a mechanism that keeps their populations hungry, frightened and obedient.

The official posture toward Neo-Bolshevism is therefore a systematic exercise in enforced ignorance. The average citizen of Oceania is not even told what the rival philosophy teaches, only that it is monstrous; and because contact with foreigners is almost impossible, no lived experience can correct the propaganda. In Goldstein's analysis this is deliberate. The three philosophies are barely distinguishable, and if their subjects ever discovered one another's conditions of life they would discover that most of what they had been told was lies. Neo-Bolshevism functions less as a real alternative than as a screen onto which Oceania projects everything it must hate in order to believe in itself.

## Historical Allusions and Orwell's Critique

The name Neo-Bolshevism deliberately evokes the Soviet Union, and the novel's historical framing makes the allusion pointed. Orwell was writing out of anger at the widespread allegiance to Stalinism among the English Left: in March 1948, while revising the first draft, he described this allegiance as producing "a sort of schizophrenic manner of thinking" in which words like "democracy" can bear two irreconcilable meanings, and things like concentration camps and mass deportations can be right and wrong at the same time. Neo-Bolshevism is that schizophrenia given a name and a geography: a Bolshevism that has been emptied of revolutionary content and turned into a badge of servitude while still claiming the socialist inheritance.

Orwell's own position, as the novel's prefatory material records, was not anti-communism of the conventional kind: he had gone to Spain in 1937 to fight against Franco's fascists, and afterwards stated that every line of serious work he had written since 1936 was written "against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, as I know it." Read in this light, the invention of Neo-Bolshevism is a warning against the corruption of socialism rather than an attack on socialism itself. By giving the Eurasian enemy a name that preserves the Bolshevik heritage, Orwell keeps the historical referent unmistakable while using the fictional trio of identical ideologies to show that totalitarianism reproduces the same structure of power whatever its label.

## Place in the Novel's Structure

Within the novel, Neo-Bolshevism is never seen from inside; it exists only as a name to be hated, and as a paragraph in a forbidden book. That double existence is exactly its function. For the Party, the name sustains the permanent state of emergency that justifies surveillance, shortages and war. For Winston Smith, reading Goldstein's book, the revelation that the rival creeds are interchangeable is a form of reassurance: it confirms that his private sense of a falsified world is grounded in an objective analysis. The book tells him nothing he did not already know, but it systematises the knowledge and makes him feel, for a while, that he is not mad.

The long-term irony is that this knowledge saves nobody. The final condition of Winston and Julia after the Ministry of Love shows that recognizing the imposture of Neo-Bolshevism — or of any of the three philosophies — does not make one immune to the machinery that enforces belief. In this sense the concept of Neo-Bolshevism points to the novel's deepest claim: the enemy is not a place or a doctrine but the structure of power itself, and a world organized into three identical tyrannies leaves no outside from which resistance can come. The phrase "Neo-Bolshevism" thus ends by meaning almost nothing in particular, which is precisely its meaning: a label emptied of content, fit only to be execrated, and perfectly adapted to a system whose real creed is the maintenance of power.
