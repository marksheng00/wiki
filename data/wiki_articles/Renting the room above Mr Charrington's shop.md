# Renting the room above Mr Charrington's shop

The decision to rent the room above Mr Charrington's shop marked a decisive escalation in Winston Smith's rebellion, transforming his clandestine affair with Julia from brief outdoor encounters into a sustained, domestic intimacy—and simultaneously sealing their fate, as the room would later prove to be a trap set by the Thought Police. The idea first crystallised when Winston saw the glass paperweight mirrored on the gate-leg table; he knew it was conscious, gratuitous, suicidal folly, yet the temptation of a fixed, private refuge proved irresistible.

## Motivation and Risk

Winston was acutely aware that renting a room for a love affair was one of the least concealable crimes a Party member could commit. His heart kept repeating 'Folly, folly'—a deliberate, self-destructive gamble. The longing for a secure hiding-place, indoors and near at hand, had grown overwhelming after the difficulties of their earlier meetings in the woods and church belfry. Though the risk was far greater than any previous violation, he believed that the room's existence would be worth almost any price.

## Mr Charrington's Complicity

When Winston approached the old shopkeeper, Mr Charrington made no difficulty about letting the room, apparently glad of the few dollars it would bring. He did not seem shocked or become offensively knowing when the purpose was made clear; instead he looked into the middle distance and spoke in generalities, with a delicate air that gave the impression of becoming partly invisible. Privacy, he remarked, was a very valuable thing, and everyone wanted a place where they could be alone occasionally. He even hinted that there were two entries to the house, one through the back yard giving onto an alley, a detail that seemed to offer an extra layer of security. This apparently benevolent discretion deceived Winston into trusting him completely.

## The Room as Sanctuary

Inside, the room was shabby but curiously inviting: an enormous bed with ragged blankets, a coverless bolster, an old-fashioned twelve-hour clock ticking on the mantelpiece, and in the corner the glass paperweight gleaming softly out of the half-darkness. Mr Charrington had provided a battered tin oilstove, a saucepan and two cups. Winston lit the burner, set water to boil, and laid out the envelope of Victory Coffee and saccharine tablets he had brought. The room had no telescreen—a fact that made it feel like a pocket of the past, a place where extinct animals could walk. For Winston, it was bliss, a temporary escape from the all-seeing eye of the Party, and the glass paperweight seemed to condense this fragile, arrested world into a single, transparent object.
