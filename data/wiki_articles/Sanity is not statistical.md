# Sanity is not statistical

"Sanity is not statistical" is the private axiom Winston Smith murmurs to himself as he falls asleep at the end of Part II, Chapter IX, after reading Emmanuel Goldstein's forbidden book. It is the novel's clearest statement of what private dissent costs and what it claims: that a single mind can be sane against the judgment of the entire world. The phrase never returns in the same form, but its exact inversion appears after Winston's re-education in the Ministry of Love — "Sanity was statistical." Between those two sentences lies the whole arc of his rebellion and destruction.

## Origin and Context

The moment comes in the room above Mr Charrington's shop, immediately after Winston has read the first and third chapters of The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism, the book he has carried for six days without daring to open. He has just understood that the book "had merely systematised the knowledge that he possessed already," and that being in a minority, even a minority of one, did not make him mad. With the sun on his face and Julia's sleeping body against his, he feels safe: "there was truth and there was untruth," and if he clung to the truth even against the whole world, he was not mad. He falls asleep murmuring the words that condense this insight, with the feeling that the remark contained a profound wisdom. The phrase arrives at the peak of his happiness, hours before the telescreen behind the picture announces their arrest.

## A Private Measure of Sanity

The axiom is the philosophical counterpart to the act with which Winston's rebellion began. When he opened the diary, nothing was illegal because there were no longer any laws, yet writing in it was punishable by death or at least twenty-five years in a forced-labour camp; he had already concluded that thoughtcrime is death and that the Thought Police would get him just the same whatever he wrote. "Sanity is not statistical" turns that fatalism into a positive creed: truth does not require witnesses, and the Party's rewriting of records cannot reach the one fact left inside a skull. The formula makes private judgment the last territory the Party cannot occupy — exactly the claim O'Brien later sets out to demolish with a dial, a cage and an arithmetic that can be made to say five.

## The Party's Statistical Sanity

The Party's whole system is engineered to make sanity a statistical property. A Party member lives from birth to death under the eye of the Thought Police and can never be sure that he is alone; even the words he mutters in sleep are scrutinised. In Oceania there is no law, only the requirement of the right instincts, and the naturally orthodox Party member is expected to know the true belief or desirable emotion without taking thought. Winston's re-education in the Ministry of Love carries this logic to its end. By the time he has been broken, the sentence he once murmured has been reversed in his own mind: "Sanity was statistical. It was merely a question of learning to think as they thought." What had been a defiant private truth becomes, in his own mouth, the Party's definition of health.

## Dramatic Reversal and Significance

The full meaning of "Sanity is not statistical" emerges only in retrospect. In its original context the sentence is an act of faith, spoken at the most hopeful moment of Winston's life; in its inverted form it is an epitaph for the self that spoke it. The two versions divide the novel's thought between them: the first insists that a single truthful mind is sane against the whole world, the second accepts that sanity is whatever the Party says it is. Winston's final condition, in which he feels that "the struggle was finished" and loves Big Brother, is the proof that the statistical definition has won. Yet the novel's appendix, "The Principles of Newspeak," is written in the past tense and in ordinary English, hinting that Newspeak and the order that produced it have themselves become history — so that Winston's private axiom may describe a sanity the future finally vindicated.
