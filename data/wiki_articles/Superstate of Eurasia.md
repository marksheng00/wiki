# Superstate of Eurasia

The Superstate of Eurasia is one of the three totalitarian world powers in George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four, encompassing the northern European and Asian landmass from Portugal to the Bering Strait. It is locked in a permanent, inconclusive war with Oceania and Eastasia, and its geopolitical role is rooted in Mackinder's Heartland theory, which Orwell explicitly engaged with. Eurasia functions as a perpetual enemy and mirror of Oceania, embodying the same oligarchical structure and ideological control, and its existence is essential to the Party's maintenance of power through continuous war hysteria.

## Geography and Origins

Eurasia is the largest of the three super-states, comprising the whole of the northern part of the European and Asiatic land-mass, from Portugal to the Bering Strait. Its heartland, as defined by British geographer Halford Mackinder, is the 'pivot of history', a concept that directly influenced Orwell's geopolitical scheme. In the novel, Eurasia emerged after the absorption of Europe by Russia, and its frontiers are partly arbitrary and partly fluctuate according to the fortunes of war, though they generally follow geographical lines. The super-state is protected by its vast land spaces, making it practically unconquerable by the other two powers.

## Political and Ideological System

Eurasia operates under a political system nearly identical to Oceania's: a pyramidal hierarchy with a semi-divine leader, a ruling Inner Party, and a populace kept in poverty and ignorance through continuous warfare. Its official ideology is called Neo-Bolshevism, which is barely distinguishable from Oceania's Ingsoc or Eastasia's Death-Worship. The citizen of Eurasia is subject to the same surveillance, thought control, and historical falsification as any Oceanic citizen, and the regimes prop each other up through permanent conflict. Orwell's critique of Stalinism and the 'schizophrenic manner of thinking' of the English Left directly informs the portrayal of Eurasia's totalitarian structure.

## Role in the War

Eurasia is the primary enemy of Oceania for most of the novel's timeline, though the alliances shift without public acknowledgment. The war is a perpetual, stalemated conflict fought over disputed territories around the Equator and the Pole, with the real purpose of consuming surplus production and maintaining the hierarchical social order. The Eurasian army appears as a terrifying, faceless force in propaganda – the 'monstrous figure of a Eurasian soldier' plastered on posters – and its prisoners are paraded through London as war criminals. The constant threat of Eurasia is used to justify Party rule, and the sudden switch of enemy from Eurasia to Eastasia during Hate Week demonstrates the complete mutability of the past under the Party's control.

## Significance

Eurasia's significance lies in its function as the indispensable enemy that sustains Oceania's regime. Without the permanent war, the Party could not maintain the scarcity, fear, and hatred necessary to keep the population subjugated. The super-state also serves as a geopolitical embodiment of the totalitarian future Orwell feared, a world where power is pursued for its own sake and individual freedom is annihilated. The fact that the three super-states are essentially identical in structure underscores the novel's theme that the will to power, not ideology, drives history.
