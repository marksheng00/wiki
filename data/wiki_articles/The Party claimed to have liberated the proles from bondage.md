# The Party claimed to have liberated the proles from bondage

The Party claimed to have liberated the proles from bondage. It was a foundational tenet of Ingsoc, repeated in Party textbooks and telescreen announcements, and it rested on a doublethink so complete that the Party could simultaneously declare that before the Revolution the proles had been hideously oppressed by the capitalists — starved, flogged, forced into the coal mines, sold into factories at the age of six — and that the proles were natural inferiors who must be kept in subjection, like animals, by a few simple rules. The claim was not a description of history but an instrument of power: it turned an unverifiable past into a licence for permanent hierarchy, and it defined the condition of the mass of the population of Oceania. Its falsity was visible in the fabricated statistics of the Ministry of Truth, in the bareness and dinginess of daily life, and ultimately in Winston Smith's own reluctant recognition that the proles had stayed human while their rulers claimed to have saved them.

## The Claim and Its Doublethink Structure

Winston, copying a children's history textbook into his diary, recognized the passage as "almost a transcription from one of the Party textbooks." The official narrative ran: before the Revolution the proles were hideously oppressed — the capitalists starved and flogged them, women were forced to work in the coal mines (as women still did, in fact), children were sold into the factories at the age of six. Yet, true to the principles of doublethink, the Party simultaneously taught that the proles were natural inferiors who had to be kept in subjection, like animals, by the application of a few simple rules. Both propositions were held at once: that the Party had freed the proles, and that the proles were unfit for freedom. The doctrine's practical content was minimal — so long as the proles continued to work and breed, their other activities were without importance — and left to themselves they were understood to have reverted to a kind of ancestral pattern of life. The claim of liberation thus functioned chiefly as a justification for not knowing much about them and for not needing to know.

## Propaganda and Fabricated Evidence

The claim was maintained not by argument but by a machinery of assertion. Telescreen announcements declared a "battle for production" in which the output of all classes of consumption goods proved that "the standard of living has risen by no less than 20 per cent over the past year," with "irrepressible spontaneous demonstrations" of gratitude to Big Brother. The Ministry of Truth's Records Department, where Winston worked, existed to give such statements a false pedigree, denying the memories of others and rewriting the past. The unreality was systemic: a Ministry of Plenty forecast of a hundred and forty-five million pairs of boots could be "rectified" to sixty-two million and then to fifty-seven million, so that the quota could be shown as over-fulfilled, and statistics were just as much a fantasy in their original version as in their rectified version. Even the chocolate ration could be announced as raised to twenty grammes a week only a day after it had been announced as reduced to twenty grammes, and the contradiction was swallowed. The story of liberation belonged to the same order of fabrication: it could not be disproved, because the records that might test it had long since been re-made.

## The Material Reality the Claim Concealed

The reality that confronted Winston made the claim of liberation hard to square with daily life. He could not tell how much of the official history was lies; the only evidence to the contrary was "the mute protest in your own bones," the instinctive feeling that the conditions he lived in were intolerable and must once have been different. What struck him as truly characteristic of modern life was not cruelty and insecurity but bareness, dinginess, listlessness — a life of slogging through dreary jobs, fighting for a place on the Tube, darning a worn-out sock, cadging a saccharine tablet, saving a cigarette end. The Party's ideal was something huge, terrible and glittering, a world of steel and concrete populated by warriors and fanatics; the actuality was decaying, dingy cities full of underfed people in patched nineteenth-century houses smelling of cabbage. Behind the boast of liberation stood the Ministry of Love, a windowless building impossible to enter except on official business, guarded by barbed-wire entanglements, steel doors, hidden machine-gun nests and gorilla-faced guards with jointed truncheons. The claim of freedom for the proles was backed, for anyone who questioned it, by the reality of that place.

## Winston's Counter-Faith and the Proles' Humanity

Set against the propaganda was Winston's slowly growing recognition of what the proles actually were. For the first time in his life, after years of despising them or regarding them as an inert force that would one day spring to life and regenerate the world, he saw that they had stayed human: they were not loyal to a party or a country or an idea but to one another, they had not become hardened inside, and they had held on to the primitive emotions that he himself had to re-learn by conscious effort. The realization came with a sharp memory — a severed hand lying on the pavement, which he had kicked into the gutter as though it were a cabbage-stalk. That gesture measured how far he had been drawn into the Party's way of seeing. The official claim that the Party had liberated the proles was a lie in the opposite direction: it denied both their oppression and their humanity. Winston's conviction that the proles were the only possible source of renewal was the exact inverse of the Party's doctrine of natural inferiority.

## Authorial Context and Thematic Significance

Orwell's invention of this double doctrine had a precise contemporary source. In March 1948, while revising the first draft of Nineteen Eighty-Four, he wrote that nearly the whole of the English Left had been driven to accept the Russian regime as "Socialist" while silently recognizing that its spirit and practice were quite alien to anything meant by "Socialism" in England, producing a "schizophrenic manner of thinking" in which words like "democracy" could bear two irreconcilable meanings and concentration camps and mass deportations could be right and wrong simultaneously. He had written ten years after the Spanish war that every line of serious work he had produced since 1936 was written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism. The Party's claim to have liberated the proles is the novel's purest fictional form of that schizophrenia: a liberation story that justifies bondage, an equality doctrine that mandates hierarchy, a history that is continuously rewritten precisely because the present cannot survive the truth. The claim does not merely deceive the proles; it is designed to make deception unnecessary, by making the very concept of a different past impossible to express.
