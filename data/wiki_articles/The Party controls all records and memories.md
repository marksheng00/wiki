# The Party controls all records and memories

In George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four, the proposition that the Party controls all records and memories is the constitutive rule of Oceania's reality. O'Brien, during Winston Smith's interrogation in the Ministry of Love, reduces the past to two locations — records and human memory — and asserts that the Party commands both, thereby commanding the past itself. The rule is what makes the Party's falsifications self-perpetuating: as Winston reflects, if every record tells the same tale and everyone accepts it, "the lie passed into history and became truth." This is the machinery behind the Party's slogan "Who controls the past controls the future: who controls the present controls the past," and it explains why the regime's power is aimed less at bodies than at the contents of minds.

## Definition and ideological foundation

O'Brien states the rule as an unanswerable catechism. Asked by Winston whether the past exists concretely in space, he forces the admission that the past exists only in records and in human memories, and then closes the trap: "In memory. Very well, then. We, the Party, control all records, and we control all memories. Then we control the past, do we not?" The conclusion is not merely that the Party rewrites history but that whatever it now says has always been true. Winston grasps the corollary during the Physical Jerks: the past, though of its nature alterable, never had been altered, because each fresh version instantly becomes the only version and therefore the eternal one, and "all that was needed was an unending series of victories over your own memory."

Here the rule connects to doublethink and to what the Party calls reality control. To comply, a Party member must hold two contradictory truths at once — knowing the lie and believing it — and must forget that he has done the forgetting. Orwell's 1948 remark that a large part of the English Left had developed a "schizophrenic manner of thinking," in which such things as concentration camps and mass deportations "can be right and wrong simultaneously," anticipates the novel's anatomy of the principle; the rule is not a legal decree but a psychological discipline applied to the process of thought itself. Its ultimate claim is that the Party's collective mind is the only repository of truth.

## The machinery of record control

The practical arm of the rule is the Ministry of Truth. In its Records Department, Winston's daily work consists of rectifying the Times and other documents so that every Party prediction appears correct and every unwanted fact disappears. Corrections are assembled, the publication is reprinted, the original is destroyed, and the corrected copy is filed in its place; the process applies to newspapers, books, periodicals, pamphlets, posters, films, sound-tracks, cartoons and photographs — "every kind of literature or documentation which might conceivably hold any political or ideological significance." Because each issue may be rewritten repeatedly as political alignment changes and no other copy survives, "All history was a palimpsest, scraped clean and re-inscribed exactly as often as was necessary," and once the deed is done no proof of falsification remains.

Behind this bureaucracy stands the Ministry of Love, whose windowless walls, hidden machine-gun nests and black-uniformed guards make it the place where intractable memories are corrected by force. The system is so complete that Thomas Pynchon, introducing the novel, observes that from the totalitarian point of view memory is relatively easy to deal with, since there is always an agency like the Ministry of Truth to deny the memories of others and rewrite the past. Even the largest section of the Records Department is devoted simply to hunting down and collecting superseded copies of books and newspapers for destruction, so that the absence of counter-evidence becomes as important as the manufacture of new evidence.

## Memory as the last refuge and the photograph

The most concrete test of the rule is Winston's memory of the war alignment. Oceania is at war with Eurasia in 1984, but Winston knows that only four years earlier the alliances were reversed; because his memory is not satisfactorily under control, he possesses a piece of furtive knowledge that official reality denies. The same logic makes the photograph of Jones, Aaronson and Rutherford the most dangerous object he ever holds: all three men confessed to being on Eurasian soil on a given midsummer day, yet the photograph, dated that day, shows them in New York. "There was only one possible conclusion: the confessions were lies." Winston drops the photograph into the memory hole, and with it the only documentary evidence he will ever touch; eleven years later he wonders whether a destroyed piece of evidence still weakens the Party's hold on the past because it once existed.

Objects that escape the process acquire an almost sacred status. The glass paperweight Winston buys from Mr Charrington is, in his words, "It's a little chunk of history that they've forgotten to alter. It's a message from a hundred years ago, if one knew how to read it." The same could be said of Mr Charrington's remembered rhymes and the room above the shop, all pockets of a pre-Party past that the rule has not yet reached. Their fragility is the point: the rule tolerates no sanctuary, and the paperweight is smashed to pieces on the hearthstone the night Winston and Julia are arrested, the tiny coral fragment rolling across the mat as the telescreen is uncovered behind the picture.

## The conquest of the inner mind

O'Brien makes clear that control of records and memories is only the instrument of a deeper appetite. He tells Winston that the Party seeks power entirely for its own sake — not wealth, luxury, long life or happiness — and that it differs from earlier oligarchies in knowing what it is doing. This power is exercised over the mind: "Power is in inflicting pain and humiliation. Power is in tearing human minds to pieces and putting them together again in new shapes of your own choosing." The interrogation at the Ministry of Love is therefore not punishment but conversion; O'Brien announces that no one brought there ever leaves uncured, and that even Jones, Aaronson and Rutherford were worn down until they loved Big Brother.

The endpoint of the rule is Winston's own conversion. After months in the Ministry of Love he accepts that two and two make five, that Oceania has always been at war with Eastasia, and that the photograph never existed; he learns to perform crimestop on himself and to hide his hatred even from himself. In the Chestnut Tree Café, watching the bulletin of a Eurasian defeat, he finally understands the smile under Big Brother's moustache and reflects that he has "won the victory over himself" — he loves Big Brother. The Party's control of all records and memories thus completes itself: the past is what the Party says it was, and the inner life, the few cubic centimetres inside the skull that Winston once thought his own, is brought into line with the official truth.

## Historical roots and real-world resonance

The rule has a specific genealogy in Orwell's own politics. Pynchon notes that Nineteen Eighty-Four was not intended as the anti-communist tract it was marketed as; Orwell had gone to Spain to fight Franco, and he later wrote that "Every line of serious work that I have written since 1936 has been written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, as I know it." The novel's analysis of controlled memory grew out of Orwell's fury at the English Left's acceptance of Stalinism in the face of overwhelming evidence; his March 1948 letter describes a "schizophrenic manner of thinking" in which such things as concentration camps and mass deportations "can be right and wrong simultaneously." That doubleness is the psychological substrate of the Party's rule, and the reason why controlling records and memories is not a side effect of total power but its central project.

Seen from the decades after the novel's publication, Pynchon treats the principle as prophecy rather than prediction: government employees paid to debase history and annihilate the past on a daily basis, a war-making bureaucracy named the Department of Defense, and news media required to balance every truth with an equal and opposite one. The rule continues to resonate because it is not only a fictional mechanism but a description of how modern states manufacture consent and forgetfulness. In the novel's own terms, the Party's ultimate boast is that it controls both the archive and the mind, and the two controls are the same control: "Who controls the past controls the future."
