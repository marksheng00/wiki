# The book

the book is the forbidden compendium of all heresies in Oceania, the creation attributed to Emmanuel Goldstein, the Enemy of the People, and the secret object of hope that passes through the shadows of the Party state. It enters the novel as rumor and exits as ambiguity: Winston Smith receives a shabby volume with the formal title The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism, reads it as the confirmation that his rebellious mind is not insane, and then hears O'Brien, his torturer, claim to have written it. The book is at once the most dangerous text in Oceania and a possible fabrication of the regime that pretends to hunt it, and that double status makes it the ideological heart of the novel.

## Identity and Myth

Before it appears as a physical object, the book exists as one of the whispered rumours that sustain the novel's underground. During the Two Minutes Hate, Goldstein is presented as the primal traitor whose teaching supposedly lies behind every heresy, and the book is said to be a compendium of all the heresies, circulating clandestinely here and there. It is a book without a title; people refer to it, if at all, simply as the book, and neither the Brotherhood nor the book is a subject any ordinary Party member would mention if there were a way of avoiding it. Such knowledge comes only through vague rumors. Goldstein remains offstage throughout the novel, an exiled heretic whose very existence is uncertain; the book is his substitute presence, the one concrete trace of his teaching that the Party cannot quite erase.

## Physical Form and Delivery

When O'Brien accepts Winston and Julia into the Brotherhood, he promises to send a copy of the book and stresses its rarity: not many copies exist, and the Thought Police hunts them down and destroys them almost as fast as the Party can produce them. Yet the book is indestructible, he says; if the last copy were gone, it could be reproduced almost word for word. The delivery is arranged as an exchange of brief-cases, and during the chaos of Hate Week, at the moment when the crowd learns that Oceania is at war with Eastasia instead of Eurasia, a stranger taps Winston's shoulder and says, "Excuse me, I think you've dropped your brief-case." Winston carries the volume for six days without opening it. When he finally unties the straps in the room above Mr Charrington's shop, he finds a heavy black volume, amateurishly bound, with no name or title on the cover, irregular print, and worn pages that fall apart as though the book had passed through many hands. The title page reads The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism, by Emmanuel Goldstein.

## Contents and Doctrine

The book is organized around Party slogans: its first chapter is called "Ignorance is Strength" and its third chapter "War is Peace." It argues that throughout recorded history the High, the Middle, and the Low have formed a fixed structure; the High aim to remain where they are, the Middle to change places with the High, and the Low to abolish distinctions, and the cycle repeats because the Middle always enlists the Low under promises of liberty and justice before thrusting them back into servitude. It applies this scheme to the world of Oceania: the three superstates, Oceania, Eurasia, and Eastasia, are permanently at war for labour power and control of the equatorial territories, but the war is an imposture whose real object is to use up surplus goods without raising living standards and to preserve the psychological atmosphere of a besieged city. The book also explains why scientific and technical progress has stopped: the machine had made equality technically possible, so the ruling group froze history to protect hierarchy; experiment and invention largely ceased, and the world became more primitive than it had been fifty years earlier. It names the disciplines of this system, doublethink, crimestop, blackwhite, and reality control, and presents them as a coherent machinery for abolishing the past.

## Winston's Reading

Reading the book is the most secure act of Winston's life. In the shabby room above the junk-shop, with Julia asleep beside him, he reads in comfort and safety: no telescreen, no ear at the keyhole, no nervous impulse to glance over his shoulder. He feels the book fascinate him, or more exactly reassure him; it told him nothing new, yet it said what he would have said if he had been able to order his scattered thoughts, and it was the product of a mind like his own but far more powerful and less afraid. When he reads aloud to Julia, she falls asleep before the ultimate secret of the Party is revealed. What stays with him is the conviction that sanity is not statistical: being a minority of one does not make one mad. Standing at the window in the last hour before their arrest, he carries the book's message forward to the proles, the solid unconquerable figure at the washtub, and believes the future belongs to them; the secret doctrine to be passed on is that two plus two make four.

## O'Brien's Revelation

Inside the Ministry of Love, during what O'Brien calls the second stage of re-integration, the book's authority is broken. O'Brien tells Winston that the book was not written by a distant heretic: "I wrote it. That is to say, I collaborated in writing it. No book is produced individually, as you know." He concedes that the book's description of Oceanic society is true, but declares that its programme is nonsense: there will never be a secret accumulation of knowledge or a proletarian rebellion, because the proletarians cannot revolt and the Party is for ever. The revelation is deliberately unsettling; the reader cannot know whether the book was from the beginning a Party instrument, a genuine heresy later absorbed, or a lie told to destroy Winston's last attachment. What is certain is that the text which once made Winston feel sane is now used to prove his madness. The notion that the book is indestructible takes on a different colour: even if the Thought Police had destroyed every copy, the Party's own memory could reproduce it word for word, but only to bury its promises.

## Thematic Significance

The book is the mechanism by which Nineteen Eighty-Four turns ideology into narrative. It gives Winston, and the reader, a systematic account of Ingsoc, doublethink, Newspeak, the mutability of the past, and the permanent war, and it does so in the voice of the one figure the Party has designated as absolute evil. Its existence demonstrates that memory and written record are the battleground of totalitarianism, the same battleground on which the Ministry of Truth rewrites newspapers and the Thought Police purges anyone who might one day commit a crime. Orwell's own politics frame the text: he had gone to Spain, written against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, and complained that the English Left's acceptance of the Russian regime had produced a schizophrenic way of thinking in which words like democracy carried two irreconcilable meanings. The book dramatizes that schizophrenia by making forbidden knowledge and official falsification two sides of the same struggle. It is also the novel's most direct handling of brainwashing: the same regime that produces the book, or claims to, responds to it with the conditioning of the Ministry of Love, a reminder that the Party's war is not against enemies but against the capacity to think.
