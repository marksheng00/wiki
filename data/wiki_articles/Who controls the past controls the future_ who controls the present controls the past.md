# Who controls the past controls the future: who controls the present controls the past

The Party slogan "Who controls the past controls the future: who controls the present controls the past" is the foundational principle of Ingsoc, the totalitarian ideology of Oceania in George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four. It encapsulates the Party's method of absolute power: by systematically falsifying all documentary evidence and suppressing individual memory, the Party makes itself the sole arbiter of truth, so that whatever it declares to have happened becomes reality, and dissent becomes literally insane.

## Definition and Slogan

The slogan first appears in the mind of Winston Smith during his morning exercises, as he reflects on the Party's power to alter the past. The Party asserts that Oceania has always been at war with Eurasia, though Winston remembers a different alliance only four years earlier. "Who controls the past," runs the Party slogan in his diary, "controls the future: who controls the present controls the past." Later, during his interrogation in the Ministry of Love, O'Brien forces Winston to repeat the slogan obediently, demonstrating its status as a compulsory article of faith.

## Operational Mechanism

The Party's control over the past is exercised primarily through the Ministry of Truth, where Winston works. Every day, newspapers, books, photographs, films, and all other documents are continuously altered: the original copies are destroyed in the memory holes, and corrected versions are placed on file. History is a palimpsest, scraped clean and re-inscribed as often as necessary. The most famous example is the photograph of Jones, Aaronson, and Rutherford, which proves their innocence—Winston once held it in his hands but was forced to destroy it, and later O'Brien denies its existence. The glass paperweight Winston buys from Mr Charrington's shop is a rare survival of the unaltered past, a "little chunk of history that they've forgotten to alter."

## Individual Consequences

For Winston, the slogan creates a profound existential crisis. He knows that the past has been falsified, but he cannot prove it because all records are controlled. The only evidence is his own memory, which is itself vulnerable to erasure and manipulation. The Party's power over the past means that even his most intimate knowledge can be denied. In the end, subjected to torture and the threat of rats in Room 101, Winston is forced to accept that the photograph never existed, that Oceania has always been at war with Eastasia, and that two plus two makes five when the Party says so. His final conversion—loving Big Brother—represents the complete submission of his inner mind to the Party's control of reality.

## Thematic Significance

The slogan is the practical expression of doublethink, the ability to hold two contradictory beliefs simultaneously. It also reveals the Party's ultimate motive: power for its own sake. O'Brien explains that the Party seeks not wealth or happiness but pure power, which means power over human minds. By controlling the past, the Party controls how people interpret the present and imagine the future, thereby making its rule permanent. The slogan thus underpins the entire dystopian structure of Nineteen Eighty-Four, showing how totalitarianism operates not only through violence but through the manipulation of truth itself.
